For monthly material-price escalation for aluminium alloy castings, I would build an Agentic Price Prediction & Escalation Model rather than using only a single PPI index.
The key is to separate raw-material market movement from foundry/conversion cost movement.
Recommended architecture
1. External market agents
•	US PPI Agent — retrieves the relevant BLS Producer Price Index series. BLS provides both industry and commodity PPI databases. 
•	Aluminium/LME Agent — retrieves monthly aluminium benchmark prices. LME describes its aluminium price as a global reference used in physical contracts and indexation. 
•	Aluminium Alloy Agent — use LME Aluminium Alloy where applicable; LME publishes aluminium-alloy prices separately. 
•	FX Agent — USD/INR or relevant customer currency.
•	Energy Agent — natural gas/electricity if casting conversion cost is material.
•	Scrap/secondary alloy Agent — important if your casting uses significant recycled aluminium.
•	Freight Agent — optional for imported material.
2. Material-price intelligence agent
For each casting part:
Predicted Material Price(t)
= Base Price
× Aluminium Market Factor
× Alloy Factor
× PPI Factor
× FX Factor
× Energy Factor
× Other Cost Factors
But I would not simply multiply all indexes together. Instead, estimate the historical contribution of each driver.
For example:
Driver	Weight
LME Aluminium	45%
Aluminium Alloy/secondary metal	20%
US PPI	15%
USD/INR	10%
Energy	5%
Other	5%
The weights should be learned from your historical supplier/material-price data, not manually fixed.
3. ML prediction layer
I would use a hybrid model:
Baseline
•	Elastic Net / XGBoost
•	Lagged PPI
•	LME aluminium
•	alloy price
•	FX
•	energy
•	1M/3M rolling averages
•	MoM changes
•	YoY changes
Advanced
•	LightGBM/XGBoost for nonlinear relationships
•	Time-series model for trend/seasonality
•	Quantile regression for P10/P50/P90
This gives you something much more useful than a single forecast:
August material price = $3.42/kg
P10 = $3.28
P50 = $3.42
P90 = $3.61
Expected MoM increase = +4.8%
Confidence = 82%

The important part: learn the price formula
Suppose your historical data looks like:
Month	Casting Price	LME Al	Alloy	PPI	USD/INR
Jan	4.10	2,200	2,250	100	83
Feb	4.16	2,280	2,310	101	83.2
Mar	4.23	2,350	2,390	101.4	83.5
…	…	…	…	…	…
The model learns:
Δ Casting Price =
β1 × Δ LME Aluminium
+ β2 × Δ Alloy Price
+ β3 × Δ PPI
+ β4 × Δ FX
+ β5 × Δ Energy
+ β6 × Previous Month Price
This is particularly powerful because you can explain why the price moved.
For example:
September price increase: +5.2%
• LME aluminium: +2.8% impact
• Alloy premium: +1.4%
• PPI: +0.6%
• FX: +0.3%
• Energy: +0.1%
• Model residual: 0.0%
That is much more executive-friendly than saying “AI predicted a 5.2% increase.”
5. Add an Agentic “Challenge” mechanism
This is where the solution becomes genuinely agentic.
Have three agents independently assess the price:
Market Agent:
“Commodity indicators suggest +3.8%.”
ML Agent:
“Historical relationship suggests +4.6%.”
Commercial Agent:
“Supplier quote indicates +6.2%.”
Then a Price Governance Agent investigates the difference.
If:
Supplier increase − ML forecast > tolerance
then trigger:
“Supplier price increase requires validation.”
The agent asks:
•	Is there a new alloy surcharge?
•	Has scrap premium changed?
•	Has energy cost changed?
•	Is there a lag between LME and supplier pricing?
•	Has the casting yield changed?
•	Has machining/conversion cost changed?
•	Is the supplier applying an FX adjustment?
6. Most important: use lagged indexes
Don’t assume the current month’s LME/PPI immediately affects the casting price.
Your model should test:
•	PPI(t)
•	PPI(t-1)
•	PPI(t-2)
•	LME(t)
•	LME(t-1)
•	LME 3M average
•	Alloy(t-1)
•	3M rolling average
The model can discover that, for example:
Casting price responds primarily to 1-month-lagged LME + 2-month-lagged PPI.
That would make your monthly price prediction significantly more realistic.
LME itself provides monthly average settlement-price data, which is particularly useful for this type of model rather than relying on a single daily observation.
